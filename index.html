A Story-Based Visualisation About How National Factors Shape Billionaires in
Australia, Malaysia & The World

Section 01 · Global

Where Are the Billionaires?

Billionaires are not evenly distributed across the globe. The United States and China alone account for nearly half of the world's billionaires. Malaysia has just 11 compared to Australia's 43 — despite both being high-income economies in the Asia-Pacific region.

Section 02 · Malaysia

Malaysia's Billionaire Landscape

Malaysia's billionaires are overwhelmingly concentrated in the Klang Valley — Kuala Lumpur and Selangor dominate. Wealth is as geographically concentrated as it is economically.

The majority of Malaysian billionaires operate out of Kuala Lumpur and Selangor. Robert Kuok, Malaysia's wealthiest at $12.1B, built his fortune through sugar, shipping and hospitality.

Unlike Australia, where billionaires span multiple states, Malaysia's wealth is heavily centralised in the western corridor of Peninsular Malaysia.

Section 03 · Australia

Australia's Billionaire Landscape

Australia's 47 billionaires are spread across its states — from mining magnates in Western Australia to tech founders in New South Wales and Victoria. Geography reflects industry.

Western Australia is home to Gina Rinehart and Andrew Forrest — both mining billionaires whose fortunes rival the GDP of small nations.

New South Wales houses Mike Cannon-Brookes, Scott Farquhar and Melanie Perkins — all under 50, representing a new wave of technology-driven wealth.

Section 03.5 · Change Over Time

Who Gained? Who Lost?

Between 2023 and 2025, Malaysia saw the most dramatic proportional rise — from 11 to 19 billionaires, a jump of 73%. Australia grew modestly from 43 to 47.

This two-year window reveals how quickly the billionaire landscape can shift.

Rapid Landscape Shift

A sudden 73% surge in just two years highlights a rapidly shifting economic ceiling in Malaysia.

Section 04 · Industry

Which Industries Produce Billionaires?

Australia's wealth is grounded on its soil. Metals & Mining leads with 10 billionaires, reflecting a resource-rich economy.

Malaysia tells a different story: Finance & Investments at 5, Real Estate at 3. Malaysia has zero technology billionaires.

The heatmap shows the same data from a different angle — darker cells indicate more billionaires in that industry. Australia dominates across most categories while Malaysia's billionaire base concentrates in just a few sectors.

Section 05 · National Factors

Does National Wealth Matter?

GDP alone does not determine how many billionaires a country produces. Singapore, with a much smaller economy than Malaysia, houses 46 billionaires — more than four times Malaysia's count.

Despite decades of growth, Malaysia remains an outlier compared to peers of similar GDP size.

The Outlier

Singapore defies the trend, generating outsized billionaire wealth despite a smaller economic footprint.

Singapore is an outlier with 8 billionaires per million people despite a moderate tax rate. Malaysia's low tertiary education enrollment of 45% compared to Australia's 113% may partly explain the gap.

Section 06 · Demographics

How Old Are They?

The Tech Generation

Australia's wealth is actively transferring to younger founders, while Malaysia's remains locked in the older generation.

Malaysian billionaires are much older — the median age is 71, and the youngest is 45. Wealth has not yet passed to the next generation.

Australia shows far more generational spread, with billionaires as young as 35. Melanie Perkins and Mike Cannon-Brookes represent technology-driven wealth.

Section 07 · Gender & Origin

Who Are They?

Billionaires in both countries are overwhelmingly male. Malaysia's three female billionaires are all daughters of the same man — Teh Hong Piow, founder of Public Bank.

Malaysia's self-made rate of 55% is lower than Australia's 67%, suggesting inherited wealth plays a larger role.

Section 08 · Wealth Depth

How Much Do They Actually Have?

The vast majority of billionaires sit in the $1–2B bracket. Being a billionaire does not mean being equally wealthy — most are closer to the floor than the ceiling.
